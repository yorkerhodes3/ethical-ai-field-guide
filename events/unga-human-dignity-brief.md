# UNGA / Center for Human Dignity speaking brief

## Core proposition

**Human dignity must be translated into decision power.**

Dignity is not satisfied by benevolent intent, a high-performing model, or a
statement that people remain "at the center." It becomes operational when
people have enforceable rights, intelligible institutions, meaningful voice,
the ability to contest consequential decisions, and remedy after harm.

## Seven-minute intervention architecture

### 0:00-0:45 - Begin with the choice

> We are often told that artificial intelligence is inevitable. Capability
> progress may be difficult to coordinate, but institutions are not acts of
> nature. Every system reaches people through choices about purpose, capital,
> data, labor, ownership, law, and public authority. The question is not
> whether we will govern AI. These choices already do. The question is whether
> they will be accountable to human dignity.

### 0:45-1:45 - Define dignity as more than protection from error

> A perfectly accurate system can pursue an illegitimate purpose. A useful
> system can still make a person an object of administration. Dignity requires
> that no one become merely a data source, risk score, target, labor input, or
> recipient of a decision they cannot challenge.

Bridge [*What Is Ethical AI?*](https://yorkerhodes3.github.io/pageturn-book/book/what-is-ethical-ai/2026-07/chapters/executive-summary/)
to [*Magnifica Humanitas*](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html):
ethics is institutional across the lifecycle, and technology must be judged by
whether it makes life more human, not only more efficient.

### 1:45-2:40 - Name the power shift

> The infrastructure that sets conditions for work, visibility, access, and
> participation is often controlled by private actors whose resources exceed
> those of many states. Calling an AI "a tool" does not dissolve this power. It
> asks us to identify who chose its purpose, who can change it, and who must
> answer for the consequences.

Reference *Magnifica Humanitas* §§71 and 95-96: subsidiarity now applies to
major economic and technological actors exercising de facto power; digital
infrastructure should foster participation, protect vulnerable people, and
remain subject to public oversight.

### 2:40-3:35 - Refuse the forced choice between present harm and future risk

> We do not have to choose between people harmed by systems today and people
> threatened by more capable systems tomorrow. The same institutional tests
> connect both: who controls capability, who bears risk, what is visible, what
> can be stopped, and who has remedy.

Fast and slow futures require a rights floor now, stronger public capacity,
and institutions that can learn and act under uncertainty.

### 3:35-5:40 - Five commitments

#### 1. Rights and remedy across the lifecycle

Human rights are the floor from problem selection and data collection through
deployment, monitoring, and retirement. Every consequential use needs a named
duty bearer, an appeal path, and repair.

#### 2. Participation before purpose is fixed

People affected by a system must help define the problem and legitimate ends,
not merely test an interface after procurement. Participation should carry
decision rights, resources, and protection from retaliation.

#### 3. Reserved human authority

Societies should identify decisions and relationships in which responsibility
must remain human: care, public judgment, coercive force, moral accountability,
and other domains selected through democratic deliberation. "Human Reserved"
is a question for public institutions, not only employers.

#### 4. Independent oversight and public technical capacity

Transparency must support action. Independent evaluators need access,
expertise, resources, and enforcement authority. States and international
institutions need enough technical capacity not to outsource public judgment
to the firms they govern.

#### 5. Multilateral floors with plural implementation

The [UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en)
can connect evidence, rights, and political deliberation. A universal floor
must be paired with meaningful participation by countries and communities
that do not control frontier models or compute. Global cooperation should
increase their agency, not ask them to accept rules written elsewhere.

### 5:40-6:35 - Make education part of governance

> Education is not merely workforce adaptation. It is where people learn to
> ask who benefits, verify a claim, live with disagreement, and decide when a
> technology should not be used. The ability to withhold delegation is a form
> of digital literacy.

Reference *Magnifica Humanitas* §§139-147, especially its claim that education
must preserve time for patient inquiry, fact verification, critical thought,
and trustworthy relationships.

### 6:35-7:00 - Close with authorship

> We should judge AI governance by a simple standard: does it leave people and
> peoples more capable of authoring their common life? Dignity without agency
> is ceremonial. Agency without institutions is fragile. Our task is to build
> rights, participation, public capacity, and reversibility deeply enough that
> humanity remains more than the audience for a future designed by others.

## Three-minute version

> Artificial intelligence reaches people through choices about purpose,
> capital, data, labor, ownership, and law. Those choices already govern. The
> question is whether they are accountable to human dignity.
>
> Dignity is more than protection from model error. A perfectly accurate
> system can pursue an illegitimate purpose. A useful system can still reduce
> a person to a score or a decision they cannot challenge. Dignity becomes
> operational through enforceable rights, meaningful voice, contestability,
> and remedy.
>
> I propose five commitments. First, apply human rights across the full
> lifecycle, beginning with the choice of problem. Second, involve affected
> people before the purpose is fixed, with real decision power. Third, reserve
> human authority in domains of care, public judgment, coercion, and moral
> responsibility. Fourth, give independent oversight the access and resources
> to act, while building public technical capacity. Fifth, create multilateral
> floors through the United Nations while ensuring that countries and
> communities outside the frontier are authors, not rule-takers.
>
> We should not choose between present harms and future risks. The same
> questions connect them: who controls capability, who bears risk, what is
> visible, what can be stopped, and who receives remedy.
>
> Our standard should be simple: does an AI arrangement leave people and
> peoples more capable of authoring their common life? Dignity without agency
> is ceremonial. Our task is to build institutions strong enough that humanity
> remains more than the audience for a future designed by others.

## Five policy asks in diplomatic language

1. **Establish a human-rights and remedy floor** for consequential AI uses,
   including notice, meaningful explanation, appeal, correction, and access
   to human review with actual authority.
2. **Fund participation and capacity**, especially for affected communities,
   civil society, researchers, and states that lack frontier infrastructure.
3. **Require accountable lifecycle governance**, including public-purpose
   justification, independent evaluation, incident reporting, review dates,
   and retirement conditions.
4. **Protect human development and social membership**, including childhood,
   education, care, work, truthful public communication, and freedom from
   manipulative dependency.
5. **Connect scientific evidence to inclusive multilateral deliberation**
   through the UN's emerging Panel and Dialogue, with transparent treatment
   of uncertainty and conflicts of interest.

## DAPPR translation for a multilateral audience

| Lens | Diplomatic formulation |
| --- | --- |
| Dignity | Equal, inherent worth establishes protections that aggregate benefit cannot erase |
| Agency | People need capability, voice, contest, refusal where feasible, and remedy |
| Plurality | Universal rights coexist with diverse knowledge, contexts, and democratic pathways |
| Power | Governance must address infrastructure, ownership, agenda setting, and distribution - not only model behavior |
| Reversibility | Monitoring, pauses, sunset clauses, correction, and institutional learning preserve human choice |

State clearly that DAPPR is a project synthesis rather than UN terminology.

## Anticipated questions

### Does this approach slow beneficial innovation?

It distinguishes delay from governability. Clear duties, evidence thresholds,
and remedy can speed trustworthy uses while preventing institutions from
externalizing risk. Some restraint is also a legitimate choice where
fundamental rights or irreversible harm are at stake.

### Who defines human dignity?

Human dignity has a universal human-rights expression and deeper foundations
in many moral and religious traditions. A universal floor should prevent
dehumanizing treatment; plural democratic and cultural traditions should
shape how institutions pursue fuller accounts of human flourishing.

### Is "Human Reserved" disguised protectionism?

It can become protectionist if insiders reserve status for themselves.
Criteria should therefore be public, contestable, attentive to vulnerable
workers and care recipients, locally adaptable, and subject to review. The
strongest cases concern non-delegable responsibility and relationship, not
only job counts.

### Why discuss existential risk when people are harmed now?

Because concentration, opacity, weak public capacity, and absent stop
mechanisms drive both. A portfolio approach protects people now and preserves
the ability to respond to higher capabilities.

### How does this change the role of the Global South?

It shifts from consultation to decision power: resources for independent
expertise; governance of local data and infrastructure; participation in
standards and thresholds; benefit-sharing; and the ability to refuse uses that
transfer risk without public value.

## Language to use carefully

| Phrase | Risk | Better question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "AI is inevitable" | Converts political choices into nature | Which capabilities, uses, and institutions are difficult to change, and why? |
| "Keep a human in the loop" | May hide a powerless reviewer | What can the person see, decide, stop, and appeal? |
| "Align AI with human values" | Hides who defines and enforces values | Which rights, whose values, through what legitimate process, with what contest? |
| "Democratize AI" | Can mean access to products rather than power | Who controls objectives, infrastructure, distribution, and remedy? |
| "The Global South" | Flattens countries and communities | Name the relevant states, institutions, peoples, and asymmetries |
| "Balance innovation and safety" | Treats dignity as one negotiable interest | Which protections are floors, and where are tradeoffs legitimate? |

## One-page leave-behind

**Title:** Human dignity as decision power

**Thesis:** An AI arrangement respects dignity when people are not merely
protected objects but capable participants with rights, voice, contestability,
and remedy.

**Five tests**

1. Is the purpose legitimate?
2. Who set it?
3. Who cannot refuse?
4. Who answers and repairs?
5. What can still be stopped?

**Five commitments:** rights and remedy; early participation; reserved human
authority; independent oversight and public capacity; multilateral floors with
plural authorship.
