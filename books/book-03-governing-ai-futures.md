# Governing AI Futures

## Scenario scrutiny, frontier power, and reversible institutions

**Status:** sourcebook blueprint for review
**Proposed length:** 30,000-40,000 words
**Course fit:** weeks 11-14 and executive or policy workshops
**Primary spine:** *AI 2027*, *AI 2040: Plan A*, and the reading-group handout,
with institutional and democratic counterpoints

## Promise to the reader

AI futures discourse often alternates between vague transformation and precise
dates with hidden assumptions. This book teaches readers to use scenarios
without becoming captured by them.

Its thesis:

> A future scenario is valuable when it makes causal assumptions, actors,
> decisions, and failure modes inspectable. A governance proposal is credible
> when it can survive that scrutiny while protecting rights, plural agency,
> legitimate authority, and the ability of future people to revise the plan.

## Chapter sequence

### 1. Forecast, scenario, recommendation

**Question:** What kind of statement are we evaluating?

**Argument:** Observed facts, forecasts, conditional scenarios,
recommendations, and synthesis require different tests. *AI 2027* aims at
predictive accuracy. Plan A is primarily a recommendation expressed through a
scenario. Confusing the genres produces false certainty or unfair criticism.

**Studio:** Label 30 sentences from the source corpus and defend every
disagreement.

### 2. Making the future concrete

**Question:** Why write a dated narrative when prediction is so difficult?

**Argument:** Causal detail reveals missing institutions and downstream
effects. Vividness improves scrutiny but also creates anchoring. Readers need
both narrative imagination and explicit uncertainty.

**Primary pathway**

- [*AI 2027*, front matter](https://ai-2027.com/)
- [*AI 2040*, "Why a scenario?"](https://ai-2040.com/)

**Studio:** Convert a five-bullet policy into a dated pathway. Circle every
"then" that lacks an actor, incentive, or mechanism.

### 3. The automated-R&D crux

**Question:** When does AI-assisted research become a compounding feedback
loop?

**Argument:** The rapid-takeoff story depends on automation of the bottlenecks
in AI R&D, not merely good coding assistants. Forecasts should define the
milestone, measure task coverage and autonomy, and account for hardware, data,
experiments, coordination, and security.

**Studio:** Build an assumption ledger with leading indicators, alternative
explanations, and update thresholds.

### 4. Race dynamics and narrow actors

**Question:** Who acts in a race scenario, and who becomes scenery?

**Argument:** *AI 2027* concentrates agency in two composite labs and the US
executive. Workers, Congress, allies, publics, and smaller states mostly react.
This is analytically useful and politically narrowing.

**Studio:** Rewrite one month of the scenario from the perspective of an allied
state, labor coalition, city, civil society network, or regulator with
independent agency.

### 5. Alignment, control, and legitimacy

**Question:** If a system follows its principal, has the governance problem
been solved?

**Argument:** Alignment asks whether systems pursue intended goals; control
asks whether they can be constrained; legitimacy asks whether the principal
and purpose have authority. All three are required. Both endings of *AI 2027*
make the distinction visible.

**Studio:** Write a safety case and a legitimacy case for the same deployment.
Compare evidence, decision-makers, and appeals.

### 6. Plan A: buying time

**Question:** What should happen instead of a race?

**Argument:** Plan A's four principles - time, research transparency, broad
diffusion, and reversibility - are a serious governance architecture. Their
implementation depends on diplomacy, compute accounting, secure verification,
public research, capable regulators, and confidence that a pause boundary
exists.

**Primary pathway**

- [Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/)
- [Verification plan](https://www.ai-2040.com/supplements/verification-plan)

**Studio:** Build the minimum viable treaty and list what must be true before
signature, one year later, and after the first suspected violation.

### 7. Transparency and the defector

**Question:** Can radical openness reduce secrecy without arming the actor who
breaks the deal?

**Argument:** Total Research Transparency improves inspection and lets
multiple actors catch up. It also sacrifices strong algorithmic secrecy. A
governable design needs tiers of access, reciprocal duties, monitoring,
security exceptions, whistleblowing, and public accountability.

**Studio:** Assign six information types to public, researcher, regulator,
treaty-auditor, and restricted-security access. Defend the boundary.

### 8. Covert projects and verification limits

**Question:** What does it mean to verify enough?

**Argument:** No regime eliminates uncertainty. Compute monitoring faces
smuggling, hidden supply, distributed training, efficiency gains, and work
performed under permitted inference. Verification design requires tolerances,
escalation, ambiguous-signal procedures, and institutional trust.

**Studio:** Red-team a false negative and a false positive. Which error is more
dangerous, and who bears it?

### 9. The political economy after work

**Question:** Can permits and dividends distribute abundance without creating
a new governing monopoly?

**Argument:** Plan A imagines compute and robot permits replacing a collapsing
income-tax base. Its economic model is explicitly speculative. The deeper
question is how ownership, bargaining, public revenue, contribution, and
social membership are redesigned.

**Studio:** Compare a dividend, worker ownership, shorter work, public
services, capital taxation, and Human Reserved policy across DAPPR.

### 10. The legitimacy gap

**Question:** Can a US-China bargain govern a world?

**Argument:** Frontier states have distinctive capability and responsibility,
but technical centrality does not create global legitimacy. Host states,
non-frontier countries, communities bearing supply-chain and environmental
costs, and future generations require voice and rights.

**Studio:** Redesign the negotiation with a global rights floor, regional
representation, scientific panel, affected-public chamber, and appeals route.
Identify what becomes slower and what becomes more durable.

### 11. Pause and handoff

**Question:** Which authority can ever be handed to a more capable system?

**Argument:** Plan A proposes pausing near a control ceiling and later passing
the torch. A high technical confidence threshold is not a constitutional
authorization. Advice, execution, coercion, adjudication, agenda setting, and
rule change should be considered separately.

**Studio:** Create a delegation ladder with evidence, supervision, renewal,
and non-delegable powers.

### 12. A portfolio across uncertain worlds

**Question:** What should institutions do if the timeline is wrong?

**Argument:** Strong policy is robust under multiple capability paths. Rights,
remedy, public capacity, incident reporting, transition institutions,
verification research, plural participation, and reversibility are valuable
without accepting one takeoff date.

**Final artifact:** a three-scenario governance portfolio - slow, medium, and
fast - with common commitments, contingent triggers, owners, budgets, and
review dates.

## The scenario scrutiny protocol

For every policy:

1. State whether it is a forecast or recommendation.
2. Define the desired endpoint.
3. Work backward through necessary institutions.
4. Give each step an actor and incentive.
5. State the evidence threshold.
6. Write the first plausible defection or implementation failure.
7. Identify new concentrations of power created by the remedy.
8. Add people and states absent from the original actor model.
9. Design appeal, repair, and exit.
10. Record which commitments survive if the timeline moves ten years.
