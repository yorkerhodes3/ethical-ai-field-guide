# Ethical Tech CoLab: AI, Power, and Human Choice

## Fourteen-week seminar and studio map

**Format:** one seminar plus one applied studio each week
**Level:** advanced undergraduate / graduate, adaptable for mixed cohorts
**Organizing question:** How should power be exercised responsibly when
artificial intelligence changes what institutions and people can do?

This plan treats AI ethics as institutional design rather than a checklist of
abstract principles. It moves from moral foundations to lived harms, from
participation to future scenarios, and from criticism to the design of
governable alternatives.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the semester, students should be able to:

1. distinguish observed facts, source claims, forecasts, scenarios,
   recommendations, and their own synthesis;
2. explain how an AI system redistributes power, responsibility, risk, voice,
   and the ability to seek remedy;
3. use human rights and human dignity as minimum conditions without treating
   them as the complete design brief;
4. map accountability across an AI system's full lifecycle and institutional
   environment;
5. identify when consent is weak, structurally coerced, or unavailable;
6. compare centralized, market-led, rights-based, and plural-participatory
   approaches to governance;
7. stress-test a policy by writing the dated scenario in which it succeeds and
   the branch in which it fails;
8. create a concrete governance prototype that affected people could inspect,
   contest, and revise.

## Recurring analytical frame: DAPPR

DAPPR is a synthesis developed for this course, not a framework attributed to
the assigned authors.

| Lens | Core question | Evidence students should seek |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dignity | Are people treated as ends, never merely as data, labor, targets, or risk scores? | Stakes, non-negotiable protections, treatment of vulnerable people |
| Agency | Can people understand, refuse, contest, alter, or exit the arrangement? | Consent conditions, override, appeal, human capability, meaningful choice |
| Plurality | Can people cooperate across difference without forced sameness? | Who participates, how disagreement is represented, minority-intense needs |
| Power | Who sets the ends, controls infrastructure, captures gains, and bears liability? | Ownership, incentives, decision rights, resource flows, accountability |
| Reversibility | Can society pause, correct, repair, or withdraw before harm becomes locked in? | Monitoring, thresholds, rollback, sunset clauses, remedy, institutional learning |

## Semester sequence

### Week 1 - Power before principles

**Governing question:** Why does an ethics course begin with power rather than
with a list of principles?

**Core readings**

- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "Executive Summary" and "Humanity's Relationship
  with Power."
- Bill Gates, "The turbulent AI era is here," opening through "This time
  really is different."
- Optional counterpoint: Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?"

**CoLab library case:** *What Is Ethical AI?* establishes the vocabulary and
lets students inspect how the later applied publications implement it.

**Seminar moves**

- Separate capability from legitimacy.
- Contrast "AI is a tool" with the claim that systems take on the
  characteristics of those who design, finance, regulate, and use them.
- Ask whether turbulence is a forecast, a rhetorical frame, or both.

**Studio:** Build a power map for a familiar AI product. Include goal-setting,
capital, compute, data, labor, distribution, affected non-users, regulators,
and routes to remedy.

**Exit ticket:** Name one actor with decision power, one actor carrying risk,
and one actor missing from the usual product diagram.

### Week 2 - What ethics is for

**Governing question:** What can ethical reasoning contribute that law,
technical performance, and market choice cannot?

**Core readings**

- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "What Is Ethics?", "Why Humanity Created Ethics,"
  and "From Custom to Universal Ethics."
- Mark Coeckelbergh, *AI Ethics*, selected chapter.
- Shannon Vallor, *Technology and the Virtues*, selected chapter.

**Seminar moves**

- Compare consequences, duties, rights, virtues, care, and capabilities.
- Examine why "fairness" changes meaning across moral and institutional
  contexts.
- Test the idea of universalism without uniformity.

**Studio:** Analyze one case through five ethical traditions. Teams must state
what each lens reveals, what it hides, and what evidence would be needed.

**No-AI interval:** Students first write their moral intuition unaided, then
use an AI assistant to generate a counterargument, and finally annotate where
the assistant flattened genuine disagreement.

### Week 3 - Dignity, rights, and the common good

**Governing question:** What does human dignity rule out before benefits and
risks are balanced?

**Core readings**

- *Magnifica Humanitas*, introduction; chapter two on dignity, human rights,
  common good, subsidiarity, solidarity, and social justice; paragraphs
  90-106.
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights, preamble and selected articles.
- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "Human Rights Foundations."

**Seminar moves**

- Preserve the encyclical's Christian anthropology while translating its
  public implications into rights, participation, work, and institutional
  restraint.
- Distinguish dignity from preference satisfaction.
- Ask when subsidiarity requires local control and when solidarity requires
  action at a larger scale.

**Studio:** Use the Dignity & Agency Audit on a case involving automated
eligibility, migration, education, or health. The score is a prompt for
inquiry, not a certification.

### Week 4 - Systems are made ethical by institutions

**Governing question:** Where does responsibility live when no single model or
actor determines the outcome?

**Core readings**

- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "What Is Ethical Tech?", "What Is Ethical AI?", and
  "The Rise of Responsible AI."
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework, selected sections.
- Madeleine Clare Elish, "Moral Crumple Zones."

**CoLab library cases:** *AI Model Performance*, *The Agentic Behavior
Observatory*, and *The Digital Provenance Passport* show, respectively,
constrained model choice, assumptions legible in source code, and
source-addressed claims.

**Seminar moves**

- Challenge the phrase "ethical model."
- Trace how procurement, deployment context, incentives, staffing, and
  redress alter the same model's ethical status.
- Contrast a human-in-the-loop control with meaningful human authority.

**Studio:** Create a lifecycle accountability chain from problem framing
through retirement. Every stage must name a decision owner, evidence, affected
participants, escalation path, and stop condition.

### Week 5 - Data, classification, and extraction

**Governing question:** What histories and labor disappear when an AI system
is presented as an autonomous intelligence?

**Core readings**

- Kate Crawford, *Atlas of AI*, selected chapters.
- Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein, *Data Feminism*, selected chapters.
- Safiya Umoja Noble, *Algorithms of Oppression*, selected chapter.

**CoLab library cases:** *AI's Carbon Footprint*, *Provenance Search*, and
*VANGO* connect material infrastructure, evidence gaps, and purposeful
restraint.

**Seminar moves**

- Treat datasets as institutions with provenance, omissions, and labor.
- Examine the material stack: land, energy, minerals, logistics, labeling,
  moderation, and disposal.
- Ask who becomes legible to a classifier and who is made vulnerable by that
  legibility.

**Studio:** Produce a dataset genealogy and "missing persons" register for a
proposed system. Include who could not realistically consent.

### Week 6 - Humanitarian AI and radical asymmetry

**Governing question:** What changes when the person exposed to a system
cannot walk away, vote out the deployer, or become a customer?

**Core readings**

- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "The Historical Evolution of AI in Humanitarian
  Work" and "Why Humanitarian AI Is Fundamentally Different."
- International Committee of the Red Cross or UN guidance relevant to the
  selected case.
- Optional: Virginia Eubanks, *Automating Inequality*, selected case.

**CoLab library case sequence:** CERAI -> Evacuation Inform Index -> ERCF ->
Evacuation Simulator -> ERUS -> HASTE -> Mariupol Severity Model -> *After the
Corridor*. Assign different links in the chain to teams rather than all eight
in full.

**Seminar moves**

- Examine the collapse of meaningful consent in crisis.
- Distinguish experimentation for people from experimentation on people.
- Ask where accountability runs when harm crosses contractors, donors,
  governments, and international organizations.

**Studio:** A "do no harm" pre-mortem. Teams write the incident report one year
after a deployment failed, then design the decision gate that could have
prevented it.

### Week 7 - Plurality as a theory of technology

**Governing question:** Can technology make difference a resource for
cooperation instead of a problem to be compressed?

**Core readings**

- E. Glen Weyl, Audrey Tang, and the Plurality Community, *Plurality*:
  Preface (labeled "Seeing Plural" in the shelf navigation), "A View from
  Yushan," and "What is Plurality?"
- Danielle Allen, selected writing on connected society or democracy.
- Optional critique on infrastructure dependence and public digital goods.

**CoLab library case:** *The Evacuation Simulator* provides a situated way to
ask whether a model preserves social difference or turns relationships into
fixed behavior parameters.

**Seminar moves**

- Contrast plurality with both techno-libertarian individualism and
  centralized technocracy.
- Examine Taiwan's examples without treating one national context as a
  frictionless template.
- Ask whether collaboration can be designed or only supported.

**Studio:** Map a contested issue as overlapping publics rather than two
opposing camps. Identify bridging statements that are acceptable for
different reasons.

### Week 8 - Voice, mechanism, and agenda power

**Governing question:** Does a more expressive vote create a more democratic
decision?

**Core readings**

- *Plurality*, "Augmented Deliberation," "Plural Voting," and "Social
  Markets."
- RadicalxChange, "Quadratic Voting" and one civic case study.
- Richard L. Hasen, "QV or Not QV?" or another critical treatment.

**CoLab library connection:** compare plural voting with the *Diplomatic
Simulator*: one aggregates expressed priorities, while the other preserves
rounds, roles, and negotiation records without claiming a ground truth.

**Seminar moves**

- Separate preference intensity from justice.
- Examine identity, sybil resistance, privacy, accessibility, strategic
  behavior, and ballot construction.
- Treat agenda-setting as power, not a neutral setup step.

**Studio:** Run the Plural Voice Lab twice. In round one, accept the supplied
agenda. In round two, let a different group choose the options. Compare how
agenda design changes the result.

### Week 9 - Work, distribution, and meaning

**Governing question:** If AI creates abundance while reducing paid work, what
institutions connect people to income, contribution, status, and community?

**Core readings**

- Gates, sections on job loss and preparation.
- *Magnifica Humanitas*, "The dignity of work at a time of digital
  transition."
- Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, *Power and Progress*, selected chapters.

**CoLab library cases:** ERCF and *After the Corridor* expose what financial
models can support and what human or legal value they cannot price.

**Seminar moves**

- Separate exposure, task automation, job loss, and distributional outcome.
- Compare retraining, worker power, shorter work, social insurance, taxing
  capital or AI use, public options, and "human-reserved" domains.
- Ask whether attaching dignity to employment protects workers or makes
  involuntary joblessness more injurious.

**Studio:** Negotiate a local AI transition compact among workers, students,
employers, government, educators, and people outside formal employment.

### Week 10 - Truth, attention, and human relationships

**Governing question:** What must digital systems protect if truth and
relationship are common goods rather than content categories?

**Core readings**

- *Magnifica Humanitas*, paragraphs 132-147.
- Gates, "AI could stunt our kids' development and replace human
  relationships."
- One empirical study on AI companions, attention, or learning selected close
  to the class date.

**CoLab library cases:** *AI-Powered Research Questions* and VANGO create a
contrast between generative assistance and a tool that intentionally refuses
to infer beyond one narrow purpose.

**Seminar moves**

- Distinguish centralized control of "truth" from institutions that support
  verification and trustworthy argument.
- Examine simulated empathy without assuming either that it is valueless or
  that it is reciprocal relationship.
- Ask what schools uniquely provide when answers are instantly available.

**Studio:** Audit the "communication ecology" around a platform: ranking,
business model, provenance, friction, verification, community institutions,
and protections for minors.

### Week 11 - Forecast literacy and *AI 2027*

**Governing question:** How can a vivid scenario improve judgment without
capturing it?

**Core readings**

- *AI 2027*, front matter, uncertainty notes, 2025-2027 sequence, and both
  endings.
- Selected forecast and takeoff supplements.
- Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting*, selected chapter.

**CoLab library cases:** ERUS makes uncertainty manipulable; *The Agentic
Behavior Observatory* audits model populations; *The Only Winning Move*
demonstrates that literal goal pursuit can be dangerous without malice.

**Seminar moves**

- Label base assumptions, feedback loops, conditional branches, and
  observations.
- Separate the scenario's stated predictive aim from confidence in its date.
- Identify narrative details that add testability and details that create
  unwarranted inevitability.

**Studio:** Build an assumption ledger. For each causal step, record a base
rate or evidence proxy, a leading indicator, a disconfirming observation, and
the next downstream claim that would change.

### Week 12 - *AI 2040* and scenario scrutiny

**Governing question:** Can radical transparency make a superintelligence
race governable without producing new forms of concentrated power?

**Core readings**

- *AI 2040: Plan A*, front matter, "2029: Choose a Path," 2035 pause, 2040
  handoff, and verification/transparency supplements.
- *AI 2040: Plan A - Reading Group* handout.
- A critical response focused on verification, geopolitical inclusion, or
  transparency's security tradeoffs.

**CoLab library case:** use *The Diplomatic Simulator* to rehearse Plan A with
additional delegations while preserving the disclaimer that a simulation is
not a forecast and has no ground truth.

**Seminar moves**

- Preserve the text's distinction: Plan A is a recommendation in scenario
  form, while described downstream effects are predictions conditional on it.
- Test the four principles: buy time, total research transparency, diffuse AI
  broadly, and reversibility.
- Ask whether eventual "handoff" is compatible with continuing human
  authorship and democratic legitimacy.

**Studio:** Use the Scenario Scrutiny Canvas. Every team writes success and
failure branches for Plan A or a rival plan, including third-country agency
and a remedy for people who never consented to the bargain.

### Week 13 - Multilateral governance and legitimate scale

**Governing question:** What can global institutions do that firms, cities,
and states cannot - and what should remain closer to affected communities?

**Core readings**

- *What Is Ethical AI?*, "The International Affairs Context" and "The UN
  System's Evolving Approach to AI."
- Global Digital Compact, selected commitments.
- Materials from the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance and Independent
  International Scientific Panel on AI.
- *Magnifica Humanitas*, sections on diplomacy and multilateralism.

**CoLab library cases:** *After the Corridor* and the Forced Labor Structural
Risk Index test whether global governance reaches people through rights,
information, and capable institutions.

**Seminar moves**

- Compare universality with geopolitical power.
- Ask how a global floor can coexist with plural implementation.
- Examine who has expertise, who has a seat, and who has veto power.

**Studio:** Design a two-level governance arrangement: a global rights and
monitoring floor plus local participatory control. Specify conflicts and an
appeals path between levels.

### Week 14 - A constitution for human agency

**Governing question:** What must remain true of people and institutions even
if model capabilities change radically?

**Core readings**

- Revisit the executive summaries and each student's assumption ledger.
- Consolidated DAPPR point of view.
- Student-selected source that most strongly challenges the course synthesis.

**CoLab library review:** each team selects one of the 21 publications and
shows how its explicit limit, refusal, or uncertainty should become an article
in the final constitution.

**Seminar moves**

- Identify agreements that survive disagreement about AI timelines.
- Separate "minimum floor now" from contingent preparation for faster
  futures.
- Replace final declarations with testable institutional commitments.

**Studio and public salon:** Teams present an "AI constitution" for a chosen
domain. Each article must name a right or duty, institution, evidence source,
enforcement path, review date, and repair mechanism.

## Assessment pattern

| Component | Weight | Evidence of learning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Weekly claim-and-crux notes | 15% | Epistemic discipline; quality of questions |
| Power and lifecycle map | 15% | Institutional analysis; affected-party visibility |
| Plural governance prototype | 20% | Mechanism design; critique; usability |
| Scenario scrutiny portfolio | 20% | Causal reasoning; uncertainty; falsifiability |
| Final AI constitution and defense | 30% | Integrated judgment; implementability; public reasoning |

## Facilitation norms

1. Critique the strongest version of a claim.
2. Name whether a disagreement is moral, empirical, institutional, or
   strategic.
3. Put a date and observation next to forecasts.
4. Do not equate a stakeholder invitation with shared decision power.
5. Ask who is absent before declaring consensus.
6. Treat lived experience as evidence without making one person represent an
   entire group.
7. Preserve the option to say "not yet," "not here," or "not this use."
8. End each critique with a more governable alternative.

## Course-level deliverables

At semester end, the cohort should leave behind:

- a public glossary of contested terms;
- a library of source-anchored case briefs;
- reusable power and accountability maps;
- a forecast register with resolution dates;
- tested governance prototypes;
- a set of domain-specific AI constitutions;
- a reflection on which work should remain distinctly human and why.
